The problem

The issue is not the tie. It's the replacement cycle.

The U.S. rail network replaces roughly 20 million crossties a year, and wood still makes up the large majority of them. Wood is easy to buy and hard to own: over a long track life, the same tie position is disturbed again and again.

Short service life

8 to 12 years in mainline service, so each position needs several installations over a track's life.

Repeated track access

Every replacement takes a maintenance window, a crew and network availability.

Creosote treatment

Preservatives bring handling, worker-exposure and regulatory burden across the asset's life.

No end-of-life path

Treated wood cannot be recycled. It goes to landfill or incineration.

Degrading raw material

Old-growth timber is scarce, and new-growth wood carries the shortest service life.

A railroad does not need a better tie. It needs to stop replacing the same one.

Thermoset chemistry

Why earlier alternatives struggled, and GFR20 does not.

Thermoplastic ties can be re-melted. Under sustained load and heat they can creep, warp and let track gauge widen.

Encell's binder forms permanent chemical cross-links. Once molded, the part cannot be re-melted, which is what lets a rubber-based tie hold rail geometry in heavy-haul service rather than only in yards or low-tonnage track.

Technology

An engineered composite, not a recycled filler.

A patented thermoset formulation turns crumb rubber into a structural material with defined, testable engineering properties.

What goes in

Four components. One engineered material.

Each GFR20 tie combines four constituents, with the binder chemistry, filler and fiber loading tuned to rail loads.

Crumb rubber. Engineered recycled rubber from cryogenically ground scrap tires, the principal raw material.
Inert mineral filler. Adds mass, stiffness and dimensional stability.
Fiberglass. Reinforcement that carries load through the tie.
Thermoset binder. Cures into permanent cross-links so the part cannot re-melt.
How it's made

Compression-molded. Zero voids.

A controlled sequence from tire to tie, with mixing, cure profile and quality checks developed in practice.

01

Collect

Scrap tires are collected and processed through established recycling partners.

02

Cryogenic grind

Rubber is frozen and ground to engineered crumb, cleanly separated from steel and fiber.

03

Blend

Crumb rubber is combined with filler, fiberglass and the thermoset binder.

04

Mold and cure

The blend is compression-molded and cured into a dense, void-free part.

05

Inspect

Finished ties are checked against dimensional and quality standards before shipping.

Why validation matters

Railroads buy evidence.

A geometry failure is measured in derailment risk, not warranty claims. That is why the industry sets a high bar for any new track component, and why that bar rose after earlier plastic composite ties ran into field problems.

Field history is cumulative. The Chapter 30 battery and meaningful in-track tonnage take years of elapsed time that cannot be compressed, and every additional MGT adds to the record.

Sustainability

A waste stream, given a sixty-year job.

Encell's principal raw material is rubber the country is already trying to get rid of. Each tie turns that rubber into durable infrastructure, with no creosote and a path back into new product at end of life.

The feedstock
~450Mscrap tires generated in the U.S. every year

Tire-derived fuel has long been the largest outlet for that rubber, and it is shrinking as coal generation retires. That leaves a growing volume of scrap tires looking for a durable, high-volume home.
